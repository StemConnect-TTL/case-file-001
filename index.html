Case File — 001
The Thinking Lab

The drug worked.
So why did the trial say otherwise?

240 patients. One experimental drug. One standard treatment. The numbers looked promising — but the statistician wouldn't call it a success.

The Setup

240 patients. Two treatments. One question.

A biotech company is testing Drug X, a new treatment for advanced cancer, against the standard of care. Patients were randomly split into two equal groups — 120 on Drug X, 120 on the standard treatment.

After six months, the team checked one thing: how many patients responded to treatment?

Exhibit A — The Headline Number
35%
Drug X
42 of 120 responded
vs
25%
Standard Treatment
30 of 120 responded

That's a 10 percentage-point difference. The team is excited.

Then the statistician runs the analysis: p = 0.08

But Wait

The team can't agree.

One researcher points to the higher response rate. The statistician says the difference could be down to chance. The project manager just wants a straight answer: is the drug working, or isn't it?

One number becomes the center of the meeting: p = 0.08

If this call were yours to make as the trial statistician —
I'd advise halting the programme — without statistical significance, the risk isn't justified.
I'd advise continuing — a 10-point difference is too meaningful to dismiss on the p-value alone.
I'd want to see the full confidence interval and the study's statistical power before advising either way.

The result favours Drug X. But the data aren't precise enough to say exactly how large the real benefit is — the true difference could be anywhere from about −2% to +22%.

Would you call that a failure?

Here's The Puzzle

"Not significant" doesn't automatically mean "doesn't work."

A 10% improvement showed up in this trial — but the study wasn't precise enough to say with confidence that it's real. That doesn't mean the drug failed. It means harder questions are left: was the study big enough? How large could the true benefit be? Is it worth another trial?

Knowing what you now know about this data —
Recommend stopping the programme — the evidence isn't strong enough to justify continued funding.
Recommend a larger, adequately powered follow-up study before any decision is made.
Recommend proceeding — the observed effect is large enough to act on now.

In biotech, "p < 0.05" isn't the whole story.

A statistician team isn't just there to calculate numbers. They're there to help answer: "What can we actually conclude from this data?"
